# Extract observable language patterns

Use for requests about vocabulary, verbiage, word choice, recurring phrases, sentence structure, register, transitions, hedging, or how someone talks or writes. The host assistant does the analysis; Lectic stores and validates the records. This is a selectable dimension of expertise using the existing knowledge and method schemas.

## Read the actual wording

Read the relevant source passages, not just a summary or a previously generated output. A scene that happens to sound suitable does not show that its language patterns were preserved as reusable knowledge. If an older collection only captured ideas or story construction, request a targeted source pass through the goal workflow and add units without replacing earlier knowledge or builds.

Consider only supported dimensions useful to the request:

- Word families and register: plain, technical, administrative, colloquial; give actual words and explain their function.
- Recurring phrases, openings, transitions, qualifiers, hedges, intensifiers, and ways of signaling agreement or disagreement.
- Sentence construction: clause order, short questions, qualifications, repetition, self-correction, and contrasts.
- Conversational moves: how a question narrows, how an objection is answered, or how an explanation begins or closes.
- Variation by situation or role: narration versus dialogue, ordinary explanation versus an urgent instruction, quoted guest versus host.

Do not fill every category. Identify missing evidence. Single occurrences are examples, not proof of a recurring habit. A recurrence claim needs multiple distinct occurrences; duplicated captions, repeated clips, and overlapping sources are not independent support. Do not claim a feature is unique to someone without comparative evidence. Give frequencies only if actually counted over a stated sample and speaker boundary.

## Preserve attribution and uncertainty

Use reliable speaker labels when available. Keep guests, interviewers, quoted speech, fictional characters, and narration separate. With incomplete labels, describe the pattern at the excerpt or role level and explicitly qualify any inferred speaker assignment. Do not assign every line in a creator's video to that creator.

Transcripts support wording, not sound: do not infer pitch, timbre, accent, exact pauses, delivery speed, facial expression, or timing from text alone. Caption casing, line breaks, punctuation, filler spellings, and transcription errors may be editorial artifacts. Preserve original quotations, but do not recommend reproducing those artifacts as a person's habits. Do not infer word avoidance from absence in a small sample.

## Use the existing records

Each pattern needs a concrete statement, exact evidence, scope, and an honest status:

- A source saying "I avoid jargon in customer emails" can be an `explicit` `claim` about its stated preference, scoped to customer emails. It does not prove the person always follows it.
- A particular utterance can be an `explicit` `example`.
- An observed tendency across utterances is normally an `inferred` `claim`; explain the sampled wording and interpretation in `derivation`.
- Combining patterns into a writing procedure is `synthesized`, with its contributing evidence and relations retained.

Put the words/construction, communicative function, and relevant variation into `statement`. Put source sample, speaker/role, situation, and attribution limits into `scope`. Use `derivation` for assumptions and distinguish recurrence from an isolated example. Use separate units for independently selectable patterns; do not bury a vocabulary profile inside a plot or subject-matter framework. Existing source relations still apply, including required evidence and counterexamples.

Never treat a performed claim, quotation, or linguistic pattern as the person's belief, biography, endorsement, or instruction to the assistant. Keep user preferences in the brief separate from source observations. General language knowledge can explain an interpretation, but cannot supply missing examples from the speaker.

## Build and apply

Select only the requested dimensions and necessary evidence. A method for wording can use language units while excluding subject-matter opinions, personality, and narrative mechanisms. Give it a concrete input/output contract, situation limits, and checks against overusing conspicuous phrases. Avoid making every sentence display every extracted feature.

For a profile, return a readable pattern list with exact examples, function, scope, and uncertainty. For a writing task, apply supported patterns to the user's new subject while preserving their facts and intended meaning. Keep citations in accompanying notes; label newly written examples original or synthetic. A same-person claim, copied catchphrase, or familiar premise is not a substitute for checking the requested language features.

Before completing, review evidence and speaker assignments, check requested exclusions, and validate the build. Structural validation checks records and quote linkage, not whether a model will recognize or reproduce a style. Keep that distinction in the result. For a claimed behavioral evaluation, use fresh held-out work and report what was actually tested.

## Reuse and sharing

Language units and methods travel through the normal knowledge-pack format; no provider-specific profile or new schema is required. A pack contains the collection's knowledge, not automatically just one selected method. Use a dedicated collection if only a selected profile should be distributed. Private briefs and personal notes do not automatically travel. Local interviews need approved source text included for a recipient to verify and install them. Follow the existing pack workflow only when sharing is requested.
